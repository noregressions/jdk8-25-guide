You don't need permission to start. Everything in this chapter is reconnaissance, not migration: five days of read-only investigation that turns "we should probably get off JDK 8" into a risk inventory with real numbers in it. Then eight habits that keep the inventory alive after Friday.

---

## The five-day reconnaissance

**Day 1 — Scan the classpath.** Run `jdeps --jdk-internals` (Chapter 1.1) and `jdeprscan --for-removal` (Chapter 1.2) across everything — your code *and* every third-party JAR. Run an EOL scan alongside (Chapter 1.10). Save all output; this is the raw material of your risk inventory. The jdeps output will tell you the truth — it might be smaller than you fear or larger than you hope, but at least it'll be real.

**Day 2 — Audit the startup flags.** List every `-XX:` flag and `-D` switch in every startup script, Docker entrypoint, Kubernetes manifest, and CI configuration, then run the `PrintFlagsFinal` diff from Chapter 1.5. `-XX:MaxPermSize`, `-XX:+UseConcMarkSweepGC`, `-XX:+UseBiasedLocking`, `-Djava.security.manager` — mark every hit for removal, because each is a Level 1 failure waiting for the version bump (Chapters 3.1, 3.2, 3.24).

**Day 3 — Check the build toolchain.** Current Maven with a current compiler plugin (3.8+/3.11+ as the floor), Gradle with declared JDK 25 support, Lombok 1.18.40+ (1.18.44+ recommended — Chapter 3.29), ASM 9.8+, ByteBuddy 1.17.5+, Mockito 5.x, current JaCoCo. Verify each against its own release notes, and make sure the target JDK exists in your CI environment at all. This is Chapter 3.35's territory, and it upgrades *first*, as its own low-risk step.

**Day 4 — Check the critical dependencies.** Frameworks first: Spring Boot (3.x requires JDK 17+), Hibernate (6.x requires 11+), JUnit (5.10+ for current JDKs), your logging stack. Then the libraries you lean on hardest. Any critical dependency with no release compatible with JDK 17 or later is a risk signal twice over — for the migration, and for your supply chain generally (Part 4).

**Day 5 — Run the two-phase experiment.** This is the recompilation paradox (Chapter 0.1) made into a test plan. Phase one: run your existing JDK 8 binaries on JDK 25 and watch what happens — with `-Xlog:exceptions=info` on, so swallowed failures surface. Phase two: branch, point the compiler at the target, and read what it tells you. The first compilation attempt against the target JDK is the most informative hour you will spend all week.

By Friday you have what Monday lacked: a list. Which failures, at which levels, in whose code, detectable by which tools. That list is what Part 5's playbook consumes.

## The eight standing habits

The five days produce a snapshot; these keep it from rotting. They double as the summary of this book's method:

1. **Audit startup flags before anything else** — if the JVM won't start, nothing else matters.
2. **Run `jdeps` and `jdeprscan` on the full classpath** — every JAR, not just your code.
3. **Generate the complete dependency tree** — count the transitives; know what you actually ship.
4. **Evaluate EOL status for every dependency** — "no CVEs" might just mean nobody's looking.
5. **Keep `-Xlog:exceptions=info` on during integration tests** — the single most underused migration diagnostic.
6. **Test old binaries and recompiled binaries separately** — they break in different ways, always.
7. **Automate the mechanical work with OpenRewrite** — spend human judgment where it's needed (Chapter 1.9).
8. **Set up Renovate or Dependabot** — so outdated dependencies surface as pull requests, not surprises, and the problem you just measured stops growing back.

The first six are the migration; the last two are why you only have to do this *once at this scale*. An organisation that adopts the eighth habit in particular never faces a seventeen-release gap again — which is Part 5's closing argument, made practical.
